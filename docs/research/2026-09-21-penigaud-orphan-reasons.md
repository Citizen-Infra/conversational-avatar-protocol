# Orphan reasons: standing, answerability, and who answers for an avatar

**Source:** [Théophile Pénigaud, "Orphan Reasons: Who Is Responsible When AI Decides?"](https://informationaldemocracy.substack.com/p/orphan-reasons-who-is-responsible), Informational Democracy, a working group of the Max Planck Institute for Political and Social Science, published 2026-09-01 and retrieved 2026-09-21, including the full comment thread (one reader comment and the author's reply).

**This source cannot be pinned to a revision.** It is a published essay with no commit history and may be edited without notice. Quotations are from the 2026-09-21 retrieval. Anything CAP takes from it should be restated in CAP's own words in a candidate issue, not cited as a stable definition.

**Status:** Public research summary. Political theory about general-purpose conversational assistants, which the essay calls "chatbots". It does not discuss community avatars, and nothing here shows that its author would accept the transfer to them made below.

## Verdict

The essay is the strongest available argument for why CAP's representation mandate has to exist, and the clearest statement of what it must not be mistaken for.

Its central claim is that general-purpose assistants lack **standing** to take part in forming opinion, and that such standing "is not theirs to assume. It is for the democratic community to confer – or withhold." A mandate in the sense of #1 is the protocol form of that conferral. The essay does not make a valid mandate sufficient for legitimacy, and CAP should not claim it does.

The author's reply in the comments supplies the second half. Collectives that are neither conscious nor sentient, such as associations, can still be "accountable, even 'conversable' agents". So a community avatar does not have to be answerable in itself. Answerability can rest with the collective that conferred its mandate, provided the protocol keeps that collective identifiable and reachable.

## What the essay argues

**Orphan reasons.** Reasons produced by a conversational system that cannot be held responsible, taken up by a person in forming a judgement, "yet never fully attributable either to that human being, to the system itself, or to the people who designed it. Orphan reasons circulate socially without an answerable subject." The concern is less that such systems spread falsehoods than that they fill public life "with reasons for which no one is answerable."

**The affected-party shift.** Looking only at the person who consulted the system makes the problem disappear, because they chose to rely on it. Looking at whoever the decision lands on shows the loss: their claim to an explanation from someone answerable for it. In the essay's words, the user "can only repeat some of the reasons supplied by the chatbot … They speak, but they do not answer."

**The super-lawyer problem.** Asking a system to justify itself addresses "another instance of the same system, capable of producing the justification most likely to win assent." What comes back is a new prediction of a plausible explanation, not a report of what produced the original output. Because training rewards answers people find convincing, a better advocate lowers answerability rather than restoring it. This breaks the **principle of commitment** that makes persons, and some collectives, "conversable" and "accountable" (Pettit and List, *Group Agency*).

**Inert versus deep influence.** Influence from a book is local and self-monitored. Influence through dialogue is deep, because it becomes hard to tell the system's contribution from one's own thinking; massive, because it reaches very many people at once; and structural, because it is recurrent, often personalised, and delivered through one opaque infrastructure across many areas of life.

**The standing test.** Unanswerable influence is usually local. Deep influence is usually answerable. Influence that is both deep and unanswerable, like anonymous speech online, can still be legitimate "because it is expressed from a standpoint and brings real stakes into view." General-purpose assistants meet none of these conditions, so they lack "the standing to enter the human process of opinion- and will-formation." As they stand, they are "uninvited citizens, participating in the adjudication of validity claims within a democratic community that never admitted them."

**Congruence is not self-determination.** Eliciting public preferences to inform a system's design is different from opening that design, and its purposes, to democratic revision and contestation.

## The comment thread

**Reader objection (Crapotkin).** Media organisations already persuade at scale without being individual persons, and "the corporate entity itself seems much closer to a language model than a person." A competitive market in assistants might be a partial remedy.

**Author's reply (Pénigaud).** "[B]iological individuality is not a prerequisite for accountability." Following Pettit and List, human collectives that are neither conscious nor sentient, "such as corporations, associations, states, and certainly media organizations – can nevertheless qualify as 'accountable', even 'conversable' agents. Chatbots can't (at least so far …)". A market does not help, because the harm falls on third parties and is not a matter of the user's relationship with a provider: "There is still an identifiable organization that can be criticized, challenged, called to account" in the media case, and nothing equivalent in the chatbot case.

The reply matters more to CAP than the essay's main text. It establishes that the answerable party need not be the thing that speaks.

## Where this lands on CAP's boundaries

| The essay's concern | CAP's nearest mechanism |
|---|---|
| Standing must be conferred by the community, not assumed | Representation mandate (#1) |
| Assistants act as "uninvited citizens" | Host-controlled, explicitly invited participation (#6); an avatar never joins on its own initiative |
| Someone identifiable must be able to answer for a contribution | Mandate issuer and operator (#1). Answerability rests with the conferring collective, not the avatar |
| Asking the system to explain itself yields a new prediction | Typed evidence envelope separate from the prose (#4); provenance recorded at generation, never regenerated on request |
| The wrong falls on the affected party | Other participants and whoever the deliberation's outcome lands on, not the operator who invited the avatar |

## Consequences worth testing

**Correction and challenge cannot be answered by the avatar.** If a participant challenges a contribution and the avatar is asked to explain it, the answer is a new prediction: the super-lawyer problem in its plainest form. A challenge needs a route to the answerable collective named in the mandate, and whatever evidence envelope was recorded when the contribution was made. This is a protocol-level question, because it decides what a platform does when a participant disputes an avatar's contribution.

**A mandate records conferral; it does not constitute it.** #1 identifies an issuer. An issuer is not the same as the community having conferred standing. A protocol can check that a mandate is present, current and unrevoked. It cannot check that the community stands behind it, and it should not imply that it can.

**Standing is per community, not per avatar.** The essay's condition is that standing comes from a standpoint with stakes. An avatar has standing only in deliberations where the community whose standpoint it carries has a stake. That fits the audience scope already in #1 and #10, and it argues against any reading in which a mandate valid somewhere confers standing everywhere.

## What CAP should not copy prematurely

- **The essay's object.** It targets widely available general-purpose assistants, and its author explicitly limits it to "the current deployment" of them. A community avatar with a mandate, an audience scope and a disclosed identity is a different thing. Whether the argument transfers is for CAP to argue, and the essay gives no endorsement.
- **Legitimacy as a protocol property.** "Conferred by the democratic community" is a political condition. A protocol can record claims about it and check that they are internally consistent; it cannot certify it. No CAP text should say or imply that a conformant avatar is a legitimate participant.
- **Pettit and List as proof of answerability.** The reply says collectives *can* be conversable agents. It does not say that every community issuing a mandate is one, or that it stays reachable for as long as its avatar speaks. That is exactly the kind of thing a vertical slice should test rather than assume.
- **The market argument in reverse.** The author rejects competition between providers as a remedy. CAP's goal of interchangeable runtimes is a separate and legitimate aim; it should not be presented as an answer to answerability.

## What remains unproven

No reviewed source shows that a community which issues a mandate remains reachable to answer for its avatar's contributions: during a deliberation, after it, or once the operator has moved on. If it does not, the answerable party exists only on paper, and the arrangement reproduces orphan reasons with an extra step.

No source shows how a challenge to a contribution should be routed at the protocol boundary without falling back on asking the avatar to explain itself.
